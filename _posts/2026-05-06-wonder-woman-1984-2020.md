*Wonder Woman 1984* arrived on Christmas Day 2020 with the weight of immense expectation, following its critically acclaimed predecessor. What we got was something... *different*. A film that swung for the fences, attempting to weave a grand narrative about truth, desire, and the human condition against a vibrant 80s backdrop. Yet, despite its earnest intentions and Gal Gadot's undeniable charisma, the critical consensus was, to put it mildly, mixed. With a 59% on Rotten Tomatoes and a 60 on Metacritic, the film struggled to resonate, often feeling bloated and narratively muddled. But even a stumble can provoke thought, and *Wonder Woman 1984* (or *WW84* as it's often called) certainly provides ample fodder for philosophical reflection, especially concerning the **perils of unchecked desire** and the **fragility of truth**.

## The Core of Costly Desire

At the heart of *WW84*'s sprawling plot lies the Dreamstone, an ancient artifact that grants wishes. This magical MacGuffin immediately sets up the film's central philosophical question: what happens when everyone gets what they want? The movie ambitiously posits that unbridled desire, even for seemingly good things, leads to chaos. We see a world spiraling into anarchy as wishes conflict, resources deplete, and the very fabric of reality starts to unravel.

Critics, myself included, often pointed out the numerous logical leaps and narrative inconsistencies surrounding the Dreamstone's rules. Its power seemed to shift, and the global consequences, while visually grand, sometimes felt underdeveloped or simply confusing. Yet, beneath these narrative flaws, there's a compelling exploration of **consequentialism** and **ethical egoism**. The film argues that a society built on individual gratification, without considering the collective cost, is inherently unsustainable and destructive. It's a stark warning against the siren call of **instant gratification** and the illusion that more is always better.

Key themes to explore:
- **Unintended consequences** — every wish granted extracts a hidden toll.
- **Collective delusion** — the ease with which humanity succumbs to selfish desires.
- **Moral compromise** — the choices characters make when faced with their deepest longings.

## The Muddle of Morality and Character

The film attempts to illustrate these themes through its characters, with varying degrees of success. Diana (Gal Gadot) wishes for Steve Trevor (Chris Pine) back, bringing with it a highly controversial ethical dilemma regarding the body in which he returns. Critics found this aspect particularly problematic, raising uncomfortable questions about consent that the film largely sidesteps. Then there's Barbara Minerva (Kristen Wiig), whose wish for Diana's power grants her confidence and strength but slowly erodes her humanity, transforming her into the villainous Cheetah. And finally, Maxwell Lord (Pedro Pascal), the infomercial king whose insatiable desire for power allows him to become the Dreamstone itself, promising everyone their desires while secretly siphoning their life force.

While Pedro Pascal delivers a committed performance that some found captivatingly over-the-top, the film's pacing — a hefty 151 minutes — often felt uneven, stretching its narrative thin. The character arcs, especially Barbara's transformation into Cheetah, felt rushed and underdeveloped, hindering the emotional weight required to truly sell the tragedy of her choices. The film's earnest attempt to convey a profound message was often drowned out by its narrative incoherence and its struggle to balance its hopeful tone with the dark implications of its plot.

> What we truly want often clashes with what the world *needs*, demanding a difficult reckoning.
{: .prompt-info }

The ethical quagmire surrounding Steve Trevor's return, for instance, could have been a powerful philosophical anchor point, forcing Diana to confront her own hypocrisy and the true cost of her wish. Instead, it's largely glossed over, softening the impact of her ultimate sacrifice. This highlights a common criticism: *WW84* had big ideas, but often fumbled their execution, opting for emotional beats over rigorous ethical exploration.

## A Reflection in the 80s Mirror

Why 1984? The choice of setting is no accident. The film consciously evokes the era of conspicuous consumption, Reaganomics, and the lingering threat of nuclear war. It's a period ripe for critiquing **materialism** and the allure of seemingly boundless prosperity. The 80s, with its vibrant aesthetics and underlying anxieties, serves as a fitting backdrop for a story about collective desire and impending doom.

Despite its narrative shortcomings, *Wonder Woman 1984* dares to ask us to consider the **ethical implications of limitless power** and the **fragility of peace** built on selfish gains. Diana's ultimate choice to renounce her wish, and her desperate plea for humanity to do the same, speaks to a deeper **existential truth**: that true power lies not in having everything, but in understanding what to give up. It’s a call for **self-sacrifice** and a return to **objective truth** over subjective desire. The film’s climax, with Diana urging the world to "renounce your wish," is a profoundly idealistic moment, even if its suddenness feels unearned given the preceding chaos. It's a moment that, for all its narrative contrivances, truly attempts to grapple with the idea of humanity choosing a higher path.

*Wonder Woman 1984* remains a deeply flawed and divisive film in the superhero canon. Its ambitious philosophical themes of desire, truth, and sacrifice often get lost in a convoluted plot, uneven pacing, and questionable character choices. Yet, it's precisely these struggles that make it a compelling subject for philosophical inquiry. It reminds us that even when art falters in its execution, its underlying questions can still resonate, prompting us to examine our own desires, the cost of our choices, and the kind of world we truly wish to build.
