*Black Panther: Wakanda Forever* arrived in 2022 burdened by an immense, real-world tragedy: the passing of Chadwick Boseman. This wasn't just a sequel; it was a cinematic elegy, a public grieving process played out on the grand stage of the Marvel Cinematic Universe. While critics on Rotten Tomatoes gave it a respectable 84% and audiences echoed that sentiment with an 82% score, many, myself included, couldn't shake the feeling that beneath its powerful emotional core, the film often felt stretched thin, a valiant attempt to carry an impossible weight. It’s a movie that asks profound questions about **grief**, **legacy**, and the **burden of leadership**, even as its narrative sometimes buckles under the strain.

## The Echo of Absence: Grieving in the Global Spotlight

From its opening moments, *Wakanda Forever* isn't just about the death of King T'Challa; it's about the very real, very raw loss of Chadwick Boseman. Ryan Coogler and his team made the brave, and perhaps only, choice to integrate this grief directly into the narrative, beginning with T'Challa's sudden illness and passing. This immediately imbues the film with an **existential gravity** that few blockbusters ever dare to touch. We see Queen Ramonda (a truly Oscar-worthy Angela Bassett) and Shuri (Letitia Wright) navigating not just personal sorrow, but the collective trauma of a nation that has lost its protector, its symbol, its very heart.

The film explores grief not as a private experience, but as a political one. Wakanda's vulnerability after T'Challa's death becomes a global issue, a vacuum that other nations are eager to exploit for vibranium. This immediately raises the question: what does a nation *owe* its deceased leader, and how does it reconcile its individual sorrow with its collective responsibility? The philosophical underpinning here is clear: **identity** – both personal and national – is forged in the fires of loss. It’s not about finding a replacement, but about understanding how the absence reshapes what remains.

Key themes to explore:
- **Collective vs. Individual Grief** — how personal sorrow intertwines with national mourning.
- **The Burden of Legacy** — inheriting a role, not just a crown, in the shadow of greatness.
- **Vulnerability as a Catalyst** — how loss forces re-evaluation and adaptation, for better or worse.

## Navigating the Narrative Tides: Strengths, Stumbles, and Philosophical Undertows

*Wakanda Forever* is, without a doubt, a visually stunning film, and its performances are often extraordinary. Angela Bassett's regal fury and profound sorrow as Ramonda are truly the film's beating heart; she anchors the emotional journey with a gravitas that few actors could achieve. Tenoch Huerta Mejía's Namor is another standout, a genuinely compelling antagonist whose motivations, rooted in the suffering of his own people due to colonialism and a fear of the surface world's exploitation, are deeply understandable, if not entirely justifiable. Many critics, myself included, lauded these performances.

However, the film isn't without its narrative challenges. At 161 minutes, its runtime was a common point of contention among critics on Metacritic and Rotten Tomatoes, with many noting its "overstuffed" feeling and uneven pacing. The introduction of new elements, like the young genius Riri Williams (Ironheart), while conceptually interesting, often felt tangential to the core emotional and political drama. This is where the film's ambition perhaps outstripped its execution. It wanted to be a profound meditation on grief, a complex geopolitical thriller, and an origin story for a new hero, and sometimes these threads frayed, leaving certain subplots feeling underdeveloped or rushed.

> The struggle to balance multiple narrative threads often mirrors life's own **existential juggling act**, where personal battles collide with grander societal forces, leaving us to piece together meaning from the fragments.
{: .prompt-info }

Despite these narrative stumbles, the core philosophical conflict between Wakanda and Talokan, represented by Shuri and Namor, is compelling. Both are nations of advanced technology and hidden power, both are fiercely protective of their people, and both have suffered at the hands of the surface world. Their conflict isn't good versus evil, but rather two wounded entities clashing over different philosophies of survival and vengeance. Shuri grapples with the desire for **retribution** after her mother's death, contrasting sharply with T'Challa's more measured approach to justice. Namor embodies a righteous fury born of centuries of oppression, forcing us to ask: at what point does the defense of one's people morph into an act of aggression against others?

## Beyond the Surface: Vengeance, Succession, and the Future of Power

Ultimately, *Wakanda Forever* delves into the profound question of **succession** and the **nature of power**. Who gets to wear the crown? Who gets to wield the power of the Black Panther? And what kind of philosophy should guide them? Shuri's journey from brilliant scientist to reluctant warrior, steeped in vengeance, is a harrowing exploration of how grief can warp one's moral compass. Her struggle to embody the mantle, to find her own path separate from T'Challa's, is deeply human. It asks us to consider: is true leadership about replicating the past, or forging a new, perhaps harder, path?

The film also subtly critiques the very idea of **isolationism**. Wakanda's initial stance of hiding its vibranium and technology, while understandable given its history, eventually proves unsustainable. The discovery of vibranium by other nations forces Wakanda to confront its place in a deeply interconnected, often predatory, world. This raises larger ethical questions about **resource distribution**, **global responsibility**, and whether true protection comes from absolute secrecy or strategic engagement. The film doesn't offer easy answers, presenting a world where every choice has profound, often unintended, consequences.

*Black Panther: Wakanda Forever* is a messy, beautiful, and deeply resonant film. Its weaknesses in pacing and plot are undeniable, and some audiences, especially those expecting a pure action spectacle, found it slow. But beneath those flaws lies a raw, earnest exploration of **grief as a crucible**, transforming individuals and nations alike. It challenges us to reflect on how we honor our dead, how we lead in times of crisis, and what kind of legacy we choose to build when the path forward is shrouded in sorrow. It asks us to look beyond the spectacle and consider: when faced with an unimaginable loss, how do we find the strength not just to survive, but to truly *live* and lead?
